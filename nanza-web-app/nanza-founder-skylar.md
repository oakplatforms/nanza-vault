## SECTION 01 — Origin Story

*The founding insight, problem, and conviction behind Nanza.*

### 1. What specific problem caused Nanza to exist?

People buy, sell, and trade collectibles inside Discord servers and Facebook groups every day, with no system behind any of it — no tracking, no logistics, no record of what actually happened. Social commerce is a large and growing opportunity, but the people transacting on social platforms have no structure around what they're doing.

Nanza started there: give the people already trading within their friend groups and small communities the ability to keep doing exactly what they're doing, with real organization around it.

### 2. What moment made each of you realize this needed to be built?

Tahir, one of our co-founders, was getting deep into the One Piece trading card space and doing trades himself. He experienced the problem firsthand — trades happening entirely through chat, with nothing behind them — and recognized it as a real, unsolved issue. He brought it to me, and we saw the same thing immediately. From that base problem, we extended the thesis to the broader set of problems in social commerce.

Tahir saw it first, in his own One Piece trades. That's where Nanza began.

### 3. Why has no one solved this problem well already?

Because the obvious business model doesn't work here. The people trading in these communities don't want to pay a commission — they trade with friends precisely because it's free. So marketplaces like StockX and TCGPlayer looked at this activity and concluded there was no business in it.

They were measuring the wrong side of the transaction. If 20% of trades happen inside private communities, that activity is currently a black box — and the party who most needs visibility into it is the licensor who owns the IP. The licensor owns the product roadmap, the R&D, the data. Knowing where and how their products actually move — across platforms and in person — is enormously valuable to them, even though no commission ever changes hands.

The value was never in taxing the trade. It was in making the trade visible to the one party willing to pay for that visibility.

### 4. What have previous attempts misunderstood or done poorly?

To my knowledge, no one has attempted this exact model — going directly to licensors and building them a complete, full-loop solution.

Those who tried adjacent models misunderstood where the value sits. They placed it at the consumer level, but consumers value organization and structure — and they won't pay much for it. The value in a market built on Discord and Facebook groups accrues to the IP holder, who today gets no insight into where a meaningful share of their product is traded. Prior attempts lacked both the right read on where the value was and the architecture to act on it.

### 5. If Nanza did not exist, what would the collectibles industry continue doing wrong for another decade?

Data quality would remain broken. The ecosystem is full of what we call "bootleg apps" — third-party platforms and open-source projects that scrape data rather than working with the licensor. The licensor has no association with these projects and receives nothing from them.

The consequence is permanent drift. Scraped or cloned data never syncs back to the licensor's canonical data. When the IP holder changes meta values, names, pricing, or releases new cards, every downstream copy is instantly stale. Thousands of third-party platforms end up with different source data, different pricing, and different metadata — and no path to reconciliation.

That is the problem Nanza actually fixes, by working directly with licensors at the source.

### 6. What insight does Nanza have that most people in the market do not?

Canonical data is the asset. A true one-to-one relationship with the licensor, combined with the ability to distribute that canonical data downstream, does two things at once: it makes the canonical data smarter over time, and it gives every downstream system consistency.

Our insight is how much canonical data matters — and how to deliver it through a platform that distributes it reliably at scale.

### 7. What truth do you believe about collectors, creators, stores, or licensors that almost everyone else misunderstands?

Collectors love the specific IP they care about — not collectibles in general. Playing Pokémon doesn't mean I care about every other IP a marketplace puts in front of me. I care about Pokémon, or One Piece, or Flesh and Blood.

If the data for my IP is consistent and trustworthy, then as a collector or creator I'll build on your system, transact on it, and share it. Most people think the data exists to sell things. In reality, the data itself is the most critical part — especially in the AI era, where consistent data is what lets companies scale and build creatively on top of it.

### 8. Complete this sentence in one clear, memorable line: "The reason Nanza exists is because…"

> **The reason Nanza exists is because** collectibles data is distributed through scraping and copying rather than from the source — leaving consumers confused by inconsistent metadata and pricing, licensors blind to their own communities, and developers without a canonical, reliable way to build.

## SECTION 02 — Customer

*Who needs Nanza most, why they care, and how value moves across the ecosystem.*

### 9. Who is Nanza's first customer, and why is that customer the right entry point?

**Legend Story Studios**, the licensor for Flesh and Blood.

They're the right entry point for three reasons: they're sized so that scaling alongside them is manageable; they're genuine partners who are giving us access to their community as we build; and both sides are working toward a long-term relationship — building systems and infrastructure for their community, hand in hand.

*Status caveat:* they are not yet an official partner or customer. The relationship is healthy, and converting them into our first customer is the goal.

### 10. Who is Nanza's second customer?

Another licensor in the collectibles space — trading cards, comics, toys, anything collectible. The profile matters more than the category: quality IP that people genuinely love, with a strong community around it. We haven't identified the second customer yet, but that's the profile we're targeting.

### 11. Who eventually becomes Nanza's largest or most economically important customer?

We honestly don't know yet — possibly Legend Story Studios. We haven't scaled far enough to say.

What we do know is that it won't be determined by the licensor's size, but by the depth of the relationship. Some licensors will simply index their products in Nanza and stop there. Others — often smaller ones — will do a full-loop integration: deep community engagement, custom solutions beyond the core platform. A smaller licensor with a deep integration can easily be more economically important than a large one with a shallow one.

### 12. If Nanza could serve only one customer type forever, who should it be — and why?

A collectibles licensor with a strong community, who wants to work closely with us to build sound, safe technology and community tools for their brand.

### 13. Who receives the greatest total value from Nanza?

There are two sides, and it's worth being precise about which is the customer.

**The licensor (our customer):** new insights, new strategies driven by data coming back from the platform, and tools to reach their audience that they've never had. That's substantial value.

**The community member (not the customer):** a product they can trust — safe, free to trade on, with no ads, no engagement algorithms, no commissions, no membership fees. They use it the way they'd use Discord or Meta, at no cost — but unlike those platforms, it isn't funded by their data and attention. It's funded by the licensor.

### 14. Who receives value immediately, and who receives value only after the network matures?

**Immediately:** community members. Early adopters can add listings and collections and start talking with friends on day one.

**As the network matures:** licensors. Insights compound with scale — more users and more features mean more valuable data, better strategies, and new capabilities like event distribution. The platform becomes more valuable to our actual customer as it grows.

### 15. What makes someone a perfect Nanza customer?

A strong community around their product, a need to grow, and limited internal technical capability — no in-house path to a full-loop solution.

They tend to be traditional licensor businesses: global distribution chains, retail relationships, and no real strategy around re-commerce or social commerce. They don't need to be the biggest brand. What matters is that the people who use their product genuinely love it.

### 16. What makes someone a poor-fit customer?

A weak community — or a company that cares more about extracting sales than about the community around the product.

A customer focused on squeezing every dollar out of their community is a bad fit. A customer who understands that sustained success is built on a healthy community foundation is a good one.

### 17. What emotional problems does Nanza solve?

Trust. When you come to Nanza, you're entering a community you're genuinely part of — with your friends and with people who care about it as much as you do. Feeling safe, feeling at home in the community, feeling good about the platform you're using. That's the emotional product.

### 18. What financial problems does Nanza solve?

For licensors who aren't yet software or AI companies, building something like Nanza in-house means engineers, AI infrastructure, and significant ongoing salary costs — realistically millions of dollars.

Because they're our client and simply index onto our platform, they get the full-loop solution out of the box at a fraction of what building it would cost.

### 19. What operational problems does Nanza solve?

Once a licensor enters our full-loop system, product development, research, and distribution all change. Instead of producing a flop or overproducing a line, they know what their community actually wants — and where. Demand differs by geography; a product popular in South America may perform differently in North America or Europe.

That enables smaller, more targeted deployments globally: not just scaling the business, but knowing exactly what to develop for each part of the world.

### 20. What alternatives do these customers use today, and what frustration finally causes them to switch?

Today, licensors run the traditional model: direct-to-retail distribution plus relationships with third-party marketplaces.

The frustration is fragmentation — every step of the chain disconnected from the next, for the licensor and the consumer alike. Nanza gives them visibility and control across the entire ecosystem. That's what causes the switch.

### 21. What is the clearest evidence that a customer would be disappointed if Nanza disappeared?

A licensor actively using Nanza would lose their insights, their full-loop visibility into each phase of the product lifecycle, and the tooling built around their community. Their options would be to build an equivalent themselves or revert to the traditional model. Either path is a significant step backward.

## SECTION 03 — Product Vision

*What Nanza is truly building and how the product changes the category.*

### 22. Fast-forward five years: what has Nanza become?

A trusted, safe platform for both communities and licensors — with **hundreds of licensors onboarded** across categories.

For community members: if I'm part of both Flesh and Blood and One Piece, I see content from both IPs in one place, and I can filter down to either one.

For licensors: **cross-collaboration**. Because everyone operates on one shared, trusted standard, we can connect licensors in adjacent markets for collaborations, promotions, marketing events, and cross-brand experiences.

The five-year shape is three things: the standardization layer, the growth of the communities, and the growth of the network.

### 23. What business are we actually building: software, infrastructure, social media, commerce, identity, AI, an operating system for licensors — or something else?

We're building a **social commerce platform** — and it's worth being precise about the adjacent labels:

- **Social media / e-commerce:** we blend features from both into a distinct social commerce experience.
- **AI:** we ship AI services — scanning, product deep-research tools — but we're not an AI company. We're a social commerce platform that's **AI-native**.
- **Infrastructure:** we deliberately avoid heavy provisioning, but our architecture supports multiple instances on one database via **client IDs**. A client ID lets us stand up scoped deployments — FAB Foundry, MinMax, the *Pitch It To Me* podcast — using our power-bar system and shared data. Those clients get web distribution; Nanza itself is the flagship platform with both web and mobile.

That architecture gives the business model real flexibility: the core social commerce platform, plus **custom development revenue** with stores, businesses, and influencers, plus open-source and third-party integrations (Shopify and similar).

We touch all of those categories — but **the licensor is the focus**.

### 24. Which of those descriptions is the best entry point for explaining Nanza today, and which best describes the destination?

**Today:** a social commerce platform. That's the accurate, simple description.

**Destination:** the same platform, extended deeper into custom development, custom services, and AI services for our licensors. But the entry point stays: social commerce platform.

### 25. If Nanza succeeds completely, what changes about collectibles?

Trust — across the whole space.

An important scoping note: we are deliberately *not* going after the high end. A $22,000 collectible belongs with authentication companies and brand-protective marketplaces. The volume is in social commerce: **81% of social commerce transactions on TikTok Shop are under $30.** That's where Nanza lives.

Success means we've built trust inside communities and delivered tools, pricing, and technology — with no commissions — that people genuinely enjoy using from inside a community. Build trust, build scalability, build strong community tools. That's what winning in collectibles looks like for us.

### 26. What does the world look like after Nanza wins?

Nanza is a strong brand that stands for community, safe trade, and safe experiences.

The vision deliberately narrows rather than broadens: smaller groups, private interactions, influencers and affiliates who may reach only 500 or a few thousand people — but who matter enormously to their communities. When it works, all of that composes into a high-quality experience: people love the product, build on it, list on it, and it interacts naturally with every layer of social commerce and social tooling around it.

### 27. What existing tools, workflows, or intermediaries become obsolete?

None — and that's by design.

Nanza is platform-agnostic end to end. We link, partner, and integrate with third-party commerce tools, blogs, apps, YouTubers, physical stores, licensors, and publishers. Discord, Shopify, YouTube — all of it plugs in. You can list some items on Nanza while your main store lives on a third-party marketplace.

We don't make anything obsolete. We strengthen and amplify existing systems by connecting data, product, and content in ways that weren't possible before.

### 28. What collector, creator, seller, store, and licensor habits change?

We add structure on top of the social layers people already use — Discord chat and Facebook groups continue to exist, and Nanza sits alongside them.

For collectors and creators, not much changes drastically; everything they already do gets brought together and amplified. The one habit that may shift: peer-to-peer and in-person trading may grow as a share of overall activity, because it finally has structure behind it.

The long-term posture is plug-and-play: keep your Shopify, keep your marketplace store, keep your YouTube — we associate you with all of it. What Nanza really does is **bring everything together around the content**, so you can see every story about everything you care about.

### 29. What are the essential product experiences Nanza must own end to end?

*(Re-answered from the original session, which drifted into business model.)*

Three experiences we must own and never outsource:

1. **Groups** — the trusted small-group spaces where trading actually happens. This is the heart of the product and can't be delegated to any third-party platform.
2. **Interconnectedness** — the association layer that ties content, listings, posts, and external resources together around canonical data. This is our structural advantage; outsourcing it would surrender the thing that makes the data valuable.
3. **Trust and safety in transactions** — legitimate payments (Stripe), tax documentation for independent sellers, and community safety. We take no commission and run payouts as a balanced flow; the credibility of that experience must be ours.

We measure ownership of these experiences in transactions, posts, data usage, and the depth of licensor relationships — not in take rate.

### 30. What is the smallest version of the product that proves the largest vision?

**Groups.**

The flow: you join the community because you love the IP. From there, you join groups — small circles of people you're comfortable trading and sharing with.

The decentralized small group — five to ten people who genuinely trust each other — is the core of the vision. That group may never be public or joinable, but it concentrates trust, certainty, and trading activity. To a commission-based marketplace, that activity is worthless. To the licensor receiving the insights, it's exactly what they've never been able to see.

### 31. Which product capability is easiest to underestimate but strategically most important?

Groups, first. Beyond groups: **associating multiple content items with a single post.**

Attaching several content items — not just one — to a post lets us connect resources across platforms and compose them into a single shareable thought, anchored to something as simple as a card, a hero, or a tag. That association layer gives us complete flexibility, and it's easy to underestimate because it looks like a small feature. It isn't.

### 32. How do Oak, Nanza, and Cortex relate to one another in plain language?

- **Oak** is a holding company. Nothing more.
- **Nanza** is the platform.
- **Nanza AI** (formerly *Cortex*) is the insight engine inside Nanza. When a licensor gives us canonical data, Nanza AI makes it smarter over time.

> **Naming decision:** *Cortex* is retired as an external name. The engine is **Nanza AI** (alternate: *Nanza Insights*).

### 33. What is Nanza AI ultimately, and what unique role will AI play in the product and business?

Nanza AI exists to continuously **enhance the data for the licensor**. On top of the insights the platform and community already generate, the AI layer makes that data more intelligent and more dynamic.

The enhanced data serves the licensor inside Nanza first. Over time, the same services can extend to the licensor's partners, third-party platforms, open-source projects, and community members who need custom tooling — an influencer who needs a custom solution, for example.

The simple version: **Nanza AI makes everything in the system more intelligent.** (AI publishing is a separate, future concern under custom services — deliberately out of scope for now.)

## SECTION 04 — Competitive Advantage

*Why Nanza wins, why incumbents cannot easily copy it, and what compounds.*

### 34–37. Why can't eBay, Discord, Shopify, TCGPlayer, Whatnot, or Fanatics simply build this?

> *Answered as one — the reason is identical for all of them.*

Let me be direct: **they absolutely could build Nanza.** They have the capability, resources, and talent. Technical difficulty is not our moat, and I won't pretend it is.

The real answer is that **working for individual licensors is not their business model.** The licensors already have a presence on all of these platforms — Discord, eBay, TCGPlayer, Fanatics, Whatnot, TikTok. But every one of those companies is built around consumers, advertising data, social media, or marketplace commissions.

Nanza's model is the opposite: it's specific to the individual licensor, building them an **unfragmented, full-cycle view** of everything happening in their community — and making them a participant in it. None of these companies is structured to do that, because none of them answers to the licensor.

### 36. Why can't Shopify or TCGPlayer build this?

See [34–37](#3437-why-cant-ebay-discord-shopify-tcgplayer-whatnot-or-fanatics-simply-build-this).

### 37. Why can't Whatnot or Fanatics build this?

See [34–37](#3437-why-cant-ebay-discord-shopify-tcgplayer-whatnot-or-fanatics-simply-build-this).

### 38. What would a well-funded competitor misunderstand if it copied Nanza's visible features?

That the features aren't the business — **the licensor relationships are.**

Two or three healthy relationships with prominent licensors is a sustainable business for the founding team; beyond that, it scales. A well-funded competitor could replicate every visible feature and still have none of that. The relationships are what they can't copy on a product roadmap.

### 39. What moat compounds over time?

I'll say plainly what most founders won't: **we have no meaningful technical moat**, and in the AI era, almost nobody does.

Our moat is the relationship — multi-year, potentially multi-decade relationships with licensors where we become part of how they operate. They know us personally; we work alongside their teams over long periods. And we grow their IP, which isn't going anywhere in the AI era — it only becomes more valuable. Partners who deliver consistent experiences and work closely with a licensor's team transform that IP over time.

**The moat is the relationship.** It compounds; features don't.

### 40. What becomes harder to copy every year Nanza operates?

Depth. Deep relationships with key contributors, affiliates, and businesses inside each community — and, above them, strong relationships with the licensors and publishers themselves.

Ties that deep into a community are among the hardest things to replicate. They also let us build custom technology for IP holders over time, on a platform that stays scalable and technically sound. Relationships plus depth: both grow harder to copy every year.

### 41. What network effects exist, and exactly how does each additional user increase value for others?

The network effect runs through community. If you already play a game or belong to a community, joining Nanza takes you deeper into it. You can belong to multiple communities across categories, but each one you join, you actually care about.

The critical discipline is scaling to the **right people** — users who discover the platform and arrive already invested. We optimize for a quality network: smaller and more refined, but far more valuable per member to both the business and the community, because these people stay.

### 42. Does each new licensor strengthen the value proposition for every other licensor? If so, how?

**Yes — through the shared schema.**

Every licensor keeps their own community, rules, metrics, metadata, and canonical data. But because all of it sits under one shared schema, licensors can build together: cross-collaborations, joint promotions, deals that run inside Nanza across two communities at once.

Members create a second layer of connection — a user who belongs to two communities links those licensors organically.

Putting an entire industry's data under one shared schema, while preserving each licensor's ownership, is transformational. Each new licensor makes that standard stronger for everyone already on it.

### 43. How does each new seller, store, creator, or collector improve discovery, liquidity, data, or trust?

Regardless of profile type — seller, store, contributor, collector — every member gets tools to engage, trade, communicate, and discover, surfaced especially in groups and on public community pages.

Every new member improves the network the same way: they're one more person who actually cares about the community, in a network deliberately refined to exactly those people. That's what maintains trust and safety as we grow.

### 44. What proprietary data is Nanza creating, and why will it matter?

Insights around **product listings and associations** — data that exists nowhere else because the associations exist nowhere else.

We mix canonical data with listings, bids, offers, posts, and external feeds, and let users associate multiple content layers with a single item. Over time, clicking a product or post surfaces far more than a price: metadata, trend signals, and summaries drawn from trusted sources across the internet — at the community level and down to the individual entity or product.

Nanza AI compounds this: every association makes the next insight smarter.

### 45. What must Nanza do now to make its future advantage durable?

Honestly, there's no structural advantage to bank on. What we have is **leanness and focus**: a small team with the skills to work directly with licensors and work through hard problems.

Two things make it durable. First, a few licensor success stories — those relationships fund the company and everything grows from there. Second, **mobility**: the ability to shift strategy and business model as technology and communities shift, the way we already have. You lose that at a hundred employees. Preserving strategic mobility as we grow is the closest thing we have to a durable advantage — and it's worth protecting deliberately.

## SECTION 05 — Licensor Strategy

*The case for major intellectual-property owners to partner with Nanza.*

### 46. What problem do licensors have today that Nanza is uniquely positioned to solve?

Connecting with their community through **clean, reliable data**. We make sure the data flowing from the licensor — the source — reaches people around the world consistently, and we build services and technology on top of it.

The second piece is interconnectedness with us. Licensors become part of our roadmap: monthly or quarterly working sessions on community growth, promotions, marketing, and custom solutions. Our web publishing tool lets us build marketing and sharing solutions with them, down to the integration level — webhooks and hook systems that operate directly inside other platforms.

But the core is the data: getting it right at the source and distributing it through our solutions and services. That's what we're uniquely positioned to do.

### 47. What data do licensors not currently have?

Everything happening in Discord, Facebook groups, and the other places where trading actually occurs. There's substantial trade and conversation they never see.

We won't — and don't want to — reach into those social platforms directly. We **surface insights** instead. Example: when someone groups three listings and a video into one collection, that's a rich signal — "these items matter together," "this connects to these sets, these pages." Licensors have nothing like that today. Nanza effectively wraps social and commerce tooling around their data, in layers they've never had access to.

### 48. What decisions can licensors not make confidently today?

Any decision touching the fragmented part of the chain. Once product enters re-commerce and third-party marketplaces, licensors may have partnerships — but no operational integration and nothing they own.

Ownership is the point: on Nanza, the licensor **owns access to this data**. That unlocks confident decision-making at the level they've never had — consumer behavior, re-commerce, trading, in-person events.

Notably, third-party marketplaces may want in as well: they're not capturing free peer-to-peer trading either. We intend to keep that trading free — we're just making it trustworthy.

### 49. What communities can they not effectively reach or understand?

Reach isn't the problem — understanding is. A licensor like Legend Story Studios already reaches every layer: local game stores, global distribution, the full Flesh and Blood experience.

What's missing is depth: data ownership and a full-cycle view at the **lower levels** of the business, plus consistent canonical data distributed from the top down through every system and service. That's what they need to genuinely understand their customers.

### 50. What commerce, resale, engagement, or fandom activity can they not currently see?

The trades happening in Discord, Facebook, DMs, and other social channels.

The surface reason people trade there is commission avoidance. The deeper reason is that **they love trading with each other** — they know each other, or know of each other, and that familiarity makes the whole thing feel safe rather than scammy. That trust-based trading activity is completely invisible to the licensor today.

### 51. Why would Pokémon care about Nanza?

Honest answer: **they probably wouldn't** — and that's fine.

A player of Pokémon's size has the resources and teams to build Nanza-like systems internally. They may simply not need us. That said, I won't rule out licensors at that scale — we'd work with any of them (see Q57 for the one angle where it becomes plausible). But our thesis doesn't depend on landing the giants.

### 52. Why would Disney, Wizards of the Coast, Konami, Hasbro, and Upper Deck care?

You might land one or two at that tier, but many have their own systems and teams — same logic as Pokémon.

**The real opportunity is everyone else.** There are thousands — possibly hundreds of thousands — of licensors across trading cards, collectibles, toys, comics, magazines, apparel. Beyond the Nikes of the world sits an enormous long tail of collectible IP, up to and eventually including names like LVMH — not our first target, but in scope for the model.

The rule is simple: **if it's collectible, it fits** — because the model is re-commerce of ownable, indexable things. We should add value to any licensor at any size. No single one has to care; enough of them will.

### 53. Why would a licensor choose Nanza instead of building internally?

The big ones genuinely could build it — Disney, Pokémon, no question.

For everyone else — the thousands of mid-level and smaller licensors — building this takes expertise and time that even AI doesn't erase. The strategy and system design we've already worked through is the hard part. For them, adopting Nanza is an advantage they can't practically replicate.

Our current scope is collectibles, toys, anime, and comics. Luxury goods and sneakers are plausible extensions later — not the first case.

### 54. What makes Nanza indispensable rather than merely useful?

The relationship. As we go deeper into a licensor's community — building ties with key businesses and members — the data flowing back becomes something the licensor can't get anywhere else. Useful tools get replaced; relationships that generate irreplaceable data don't.

### 55. What objections will a licensor raise first, and what evidence answers each objection?

**The first objection, every time:** *"We don't want to be tightly coupled to Nanza. We want full control of our data."*

It's a fair concern — integration means their data lives inside our schema, and that requires trust.

**The follow-up:** *"Can we use you without integrating — just scrape us?"* Our answer is **no**. A scraping-based model is exactly the bootleg problem we exist to fix, and it isn't scalable for us to support.

If a licensor holds the objection, the response is simply: *understood — the door is open whenever you're ready.* We don't argue anyone into an integration they don't want.

### 56. What does an ideal first licensor partnership look like in scope, economics, data, and outcomes?

**Scope:** a mid-level licensor with global presence and distribution — Legend Story Studios is the archetype. Deliberately not a Pokémon or Disney.

**Economics:** three layers — a monthly service fee, usage-based charges as their community scales, and custom AI, solutions, and agency-level services on top.

Taken together, that's a substantial partnership, and it's the template we intend to repeat.

### 57. If you became CEO of Pokémon tomorrow, why would signing with Nanza become an obvious decision?

Because Nanza lets a licensor of that size **try the model without building it or owning it**. No in-house development program, no direct affiliation — Nanza is its own service, at arm's length.

That's the one path where the very largest licensors become plausible: they love the technology, they want the insights, and they explicitly don't want the strategy burden of building it themselves. (This tempers Q51 — "probably not" becomes "possibly, on these terms.")

### 58. How does one successful licensor partnership make the next one easier?

The success story does the selling.

Once we've gone deep into one community — AI, services, a platform the community actively loves and engages with — the licensor has a win-win-win: new insights, new access, a thriving community. That story is the sales deck for the next licensor.

Past a certain point, I believe the motion inverts: licensors start coming to us.

## SECTION 06 — Business Model

*Where revenue comes from, who pays, and how the economics evolve.*

### 59. Where does every dollar of Nanza revenue come from today?

**The licensor.** The licensor is the customer; every dollar comes from the licensor.

### 60. Where should revenue come from in one year, five years, and ten years?

More licensors, deeper relationships, more services — but **the customer never changes**. Every new offering, including Nanza AI capabilities and custom builds, still bills to the licensor.

The three revenue shapes within each relationship:
1. **Monthly retainer / base service** — the cost of working with us; likely $5K–$10K/month, exact pricing TBD. This includes access to the platform and the Nanza AI insight layer.
2. **Usage** — scaling with community activity.
3. **Custom builds** — the retainer keeps us on call; custom solutions and AI services are billed on top.

### 61. Which revenue stream eventually becomes the largest — and why?

**Custom development and AI services through our agency layer.**

Custom work — increasingly Nanza AI–driven — produces genuinely valuable systems for larger clients, at high price points. It still flows through the licensor relationship; it just scales that relationship into new business models per client.

### 62. Which potential revenue stream is strategically least important?

I can't rank one lowest, because everything routes through the licensor — the question fits a business with more heterogeneous revenue than ours.

What I can say is the target: **three to four licensor relationships.** Relationships like that last for years and sustain the company comfortably. Everything else is secondary to landing them.

### 63. Which revenue stream has the highest margins?

All of them are high-margin, because the cost basis is our own development time. If one leads, it's **custom development** — quick-turnaround delivery on larger projects commands the best economics.

### 64. Which revenue stream has the greatest ability to scale?

All three scale — retainer (more licensors), usage (bigger communities), and custom services (deeper relationships) — and additional streams, particularly around Nanza AI services, are likely to emerge over time.

### 65. Which customers pay, and what are they paying to accomplish?

**Community members pay nothing.** They just join.

**The licensor pays**, for three things:
- **Data.**
- **Insights** into their product via Nanza AI.
- **Visibility into trading activity** that today happens invisibly in Facebook groups and Discord — a large share of their community's real behavior.

The result: the licensor gets closer to their community and connected to far more data about what's selling and trading.

### 66. Which customers should never pay?

**Community members. Ever.**

The one nuance: if premium offerings emerge, they're structured *through the licensor* — the licensor remains the paying customer even for premium. Community members never see a bill from Nanza.

### 67. What should eventually become free, and what should become premium?

Everything stays free for community members. Premium is a **licensor-side tier**: "here's what your community gets free; these additional services cost money." When the licensor offers premium services to their community, **both we and the licensor earn from it** — which deepens the relationship rather than straining it.

One hard constraint: **premium features must not be custom development.** Premium features have to be reusable across licensors and applications — powerful, productized tooling, never one-offs. Custom work is a separate (and separately billed) stream.

### 68. What are the most important unit economics for understanding Nanza?

Our economics don't hinge on trades and transactions, which makes the standard marketplace metrics the wrong lens.

The driving variable is **data and usage**: more usage helps us, helps Nanza AI get smarter, and helps our clients — who *want* their usage tier to grow. The usage line itself is essentially **cost pass-through with a 20–30% margin**: if infrastructure for a licensor's community runs $1,000/month, they pay roughly $1,300. It exists so we never eat a scaling infrastructure bill, not as a profit center.

Priority order of the model:
1. **Monthly access to the data and insight layer** — first.
2. **Custom services and solutions** — second.
3. Usage — deliberately last.

### 69. How do pricing and incentives reinforce adoption across collectors, stores, creators, and licensors?

On the licensor side: the relationship lets us build custom promotions and marketing alongside them, which drives their community's adoption directly.

On the community side, the incentive is the experience itself: quality data, quality pricing, and trusted spaces with people I know — the same conversations and trades I already have on Discord, with structure. And everything connects outward: Discord, Shopify, other marketplaces, trading that happens off-platform. Nothing about adoption requires abandoning anything.

### 70. What must be true for Nanza's business model to become both large and defensible?

Deeper custom solutions per community, and deeper relationships per community.

Operationally, that eventually means **community specialists** and **account management** — AI covers much of the work, but face-to-face time with licensors and businesses can't be automated away. So the team grows deliberately into growth, sales, and marketing roles over time.

The core requirement never changes: **relationships of genuine quality — the kind that last decades.** That's what makes the model both large and defensible.

## SECTION 07 — Growth

*Acquisition, retention, habit, and the flywheels that make growth compound.*

### 71. How does one user create another user?

Sharing. Affiliate users and regular users share listings and posts, connect with people, and onboard friends through chat and community experiences we keep enhancing.

The next major capability is **composite sharing** — bundling multiple content items together and sharing them directly with one friend. Those interconnected sharing flows are the distribution engine. The design target: **one user should create many users.**

### 72. How does one store create another store?

Visible success. When a store sees a business account genuinely making money in this ecosystem, that's the pull. Stores follow proof, not pitches.

### 73. How does one licensor create another licensor?

**Visible community success.**

There's a structural feature here: you don't wander into a Nanza community — you arrive because you already know it exists and you're part of it. So when a licensor evaluates us, what they're really seeing is another licensor's thriving community. That's what sells the next one.

### 74. How does one creator create another creator, and one collector create another collector?

Through what they make and share:

- **Collections** they build and publish.
- **Groups of cards** composed and shared.
- **Bids and listings** shared outward.
- **Posts** — including YouTube and other video — connected to assets across IP holders.

Creators see what other creators built and want to build their own. That's the engine.

### 75. What is Nanza's cheapest credible acquisition channel?

The **affiliate system** we're building now. Affiliates make the platform livelier with their content — and they bring their existing audience with them. Those followers arrive, find other content they enjoy, and start contributing their own. Low cost, compounding return.

### 76. What is Nanza's strongest acquisition channel?

**Connectivity into the platforms people already use** — Discord, Facebook, and the other social tools where these communities already live.

We'll use every channel — LinkedIn outreach, game stores, local events — but the strongest is meeting people inside the tools they're already in, rather than asking them to leave.

### 77. What becomes Nanza's primary growth flywheel? Walk through each step.

1. Keep the licensor happy, with product data distributed as accurately as we can make it — the target is 100%, and the standard is best-in-class quality even if perfection isn't achievable.
2. That data quality and relationship depth produce a visible community success.
3. That success story lands the next licensor, quickly, with the same relationship quality.
4. Repeat.

Great relationship → visible success → next licensor. That's the flywheel.

### 78. What makes users invite friends or bring their communities?

Safety, and the feeling of "this is our place." A place to share, grow, trade, talk directly, connect with events, and share what happened at them — in small groups and large ones. People invite friends into places that feel like theirs.

### 79. What makes users return daily or weekly?

**Consolidation.** My own behavior is the model: today I check three Discord servers, a Facebook trading group, a marketplace, and several YouTube channels — separately.

Nanza is all of it in one place: the heroes I follow, what my friends are sharing, the listings, the conversations. Users return daily for content creation, sharing, buying, selling, and trading in one dynamic space — and as a byproduct, all that activity generates product-level insight, tied directly to the content itself.

### 80. What creates habit? What could create genuine obsession?

**Habit** comes from interconnectivity: following a post into a product, into a listing, into an article, out to a YouTube video. The connected graph is what brings people back.

**On obsession: we explicitly don't want it.** We will not build a doomscrolling experience. We want engaged users — learning, improving, chatting, trading. Nanza should be a genuinely positive experience, not a compulsive one. That's a product principle, not a marketing line.

### 81. What are the key activation moments for each customer type?

Get people in, then let them flow. Need to buy something? You become a customer account. Ready to sell? Seller account. Arriving as an affiliate who doesn't sell? That works too.

The structural point: **account types are additive.** One person can be an affiliate *and* a seller. Activation is then a UX problem — moving people through listings and experiences until the account types they need find them.

### 82. What metric best predicts retention?

**Sharing volume** — articles, posts, and trades shared.

Crucially, in both directions: content brought from outside *in*, and content taken from Nanza *out* to Discord and elsewhere. That bidirectional flow means Nanza has become part of how someone participates in their community. That's the retention signal.

### 83. What sequence of markets, communities, or categories should Nanza enter — and why in that order?

The sequence is driven by which licensors want to work with us — filtered by alignment with the vision.

We're not doing auto parts. An emerging trading card game, a collectible toy line, a comic publisher — anything where a community genuinely loves the IP fits. Collectibles broadly, including retro/OG gaming systems, are all in scope. Fit and enthusiasm order the sequence, not category size.

### 84. What growth tactic might create impressive short-term numbers but damage the long-term network?

Any of them — and we want none of it. We're not venture-backed and don't need to manufacture growth.

The model is the opposite of viral: communities grow with the licensor and stay relatively private unless you're genuinely part of them. You don't download Nanza cold — **you join because the people in your community, at your events, are already using it and you're not.** Growth tactics that bypass that mechanism would fill the network with people who don't belong in it, and the trust that makes everything work would erode.

## SECTION 11 — Founder Philosophy

*The founders' convictions, priorities, and principles for building the company.*

### 122. What do you believe that almost nobody else believes?

That the future of community technology is **tighter, not broader**. Put people in communities they love, let them narrow into private groups and local circles, add capabilities like geolocation — and the bonds get stronger: "I'm connected to this person. I'm in these groups with these people."

The advance isn't technological; it's an advance in **how you think about community**. Technology becomes a reference layer for real relationships. And the economics follow: the experience stays free-flowing because the party who pays is the IP holder, who wants data on their IP and wants their business built the right way.

**One firm boundary:** this is not surveillance. We don't read into private chats — not even in small groups. The system must be one people trust *and* one licensors get great insight from, and those two properties have to hold simultaneously or the whole thing is wrong.

### 123. What part of Nanza excites each of you most?

The growth — connecting with licensors and building these communities, because that's what proves the model.

The economics of the proof are compelling: for a company doing millions in revenue, $50K–$100K is roughly one employee. Instead of one mid-level hire, they get this entire platform and its operations. Pricing will vary — less for a Legend Story Studios today, potentially millions per customer in the future — but either way, it sustains us.

And if adoption gets genuinely deep — communities themselves embracing it, not just licensors — that's when the next chapter opens: a platform with real value to the people on it.

### 124. If Nanza had enough money to build only one thing over the next year, what would it be?

**Finish the social integrations.** It's the most critical work, and it's what I'm building right now.

It isn't a money problem — it's a building problem. And "one thing" has to include the expressive design layer: **tagging** and **relations between content items**. That's the association capability everything else depends on.

Our answer is to make the premise false: **the data shouldn't be incomplete.**

Mutual conviction: we genuinely believe in them, and they genuinely believe in us — whether we're both growing, or they're the mature, stable side of the pair.

A small, senior, craft-driven company that people actually enjoy being part of — because that's the company we're already running, and scaling it shouldn't mean losing it.

**Rewarded:**

It's also the milestone I've explicitly set as the company's entire near-term agenda.
3. **The first licensor who comes to us.** The moment the sales motion inverts — a licensor approaching Nanza because they've seen another community thrive — is the moment the network effect exists. Everything after that milestone is scaling; everything before it is proving.

### 134. What is the one story about Nanza that every investor, licensor, strategic partner, customer, and employee should remember?

Tahir was trading One Piece cards in Discord servers — real trades, real money, real community — and there was nothing behind any of it. No record, no structure, no data. Multiply that by every Discord server and Facebook group in collectibles, and you get an enormous share of an industry happening in a black box.

Everyone who looked at that black box before us asked, "how do we take a cut of these trades?" — and gave up, because the traders won't pay. We asked a different question: **"who needs to see this?"** The answer is the licensor who owns the IP — the one party with everything to gain from visibility and no interest in taxing their own community.

That's the whole company in one move: **we made the invisible part of the collectibles economy visible to the people who own it, without charging the people who live in it.** The trades were always happening. Nanza just means, for the first time, they count.

### 135. What question should we have asked — but did not?

**"When a licensor leaves, what do they take with them — and what does Nanza keep?"**

This entire document argues that licensors should trust us with their canonical data, and Q55 says their first objection will be exactly about control of that data. Yet we never asked ourselves the exit question, and it deserves a first-principles answer before a licensor asks it in a negotiation:

- **The canonical data is theirs — full stop.** It came from them; it leaves with them, in a portable form, without games. Any other answer makes us a hostage-taker, and hostage-taking is incompatible with a relationship-moat business.
- **The derived insight layer is the honest gray zone.** Nanza AI's enhancements are built from their data plus our platform's activity. My instinct: the licensor should leave with the insights we delivered them, and Nanza keeps the machinery and the cross-platform learning. But that boundary needs to be written down, not improvised.
- **The community's own content belongs to the community members**, not to us and not to the licensor.

A company whose moat is trust should have the world's best answer to "what happens if we leave." We should finish this answer before Legend Story Studios signs, precisely so we can put it on the table unprompted.

### 136. What additional thoughts or insights should every future Nanza employee, investor, or partner understand?

Five things, and they summarize everything above:

1. **The customer is the licensor. The community is the point.** Hold both simultaneously. Revenue comes from licensors; the reason the revenue exists is a community that trusts us. Sacrifice either for the other and both collapse.
2. **Canonical data is the product beneath the product.** Features come and go. The thing that compounds is data that's right because it comes from the source — and an AI layer that makes it smarter every day it lives with us.
3. **The moat is relationships, honestly held.** We tell licensors that competitors could build our features, that they control their data, that the door stays open if they say no. That honesty is not a negotiating weakness — it is the moat. Trust compounds only when it's never been spent.
4. **We are deliberately small and deliberately unhurried.** No venture pressure, no growth theater, no obsession mechanics. The next twelve months are about five customers, not five hundred. Anyone joining should want to build something excellent with a small group of people who like each other — because that's the actual company.
5. **This is being built for the joy of building it.** I sleep well. The founders might need side work along the way, and that's priced in. If Nanza becomes big, it will be because staying true to all of the above made it inevitable — never because we chased big at the expense of it.
